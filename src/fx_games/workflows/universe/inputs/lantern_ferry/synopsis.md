# THE LANTERN FERRY

The Sennow is a slow grey river a third of a mile wide, and it separates the
only two towns for forty miles: Tallow Landing on the south bank and Kestwick
on the north. There is no bridge. Both banks are soft silt that will not hold a
pier, and the riverbed underneath them will not stay put.

From late summer to the first hard frost the water fills with flakeshell, a
drifting animal no larger than a grain of salt whose pale shells turn the river
opaque. A hand held a foot under the surface disappears. While the flakeshell
runs, the current drags sand along the bottom and the deep channel wanders; a
lane that carried a loaded boat at dusk can be a shoal by morning.

The flakeshell is also the reason the crossing works at all. Oil pressed from
lampreed burns a narrow amber, and flakeshell scatters that amber differently
over deep water than over sand. Seen from a masthead, the deep channel reads as
a long unbroken amber column lying on the surface, and a shoal reads as a short
broken green one. Reading those two colours is the whole skill of the crossing.
It can be done only after dark, only from above the water, and only with one
light burning.

The rest of the year the river is a different problem. In spring the water runs
clear and low and three gravel fords open upstream of Tallow Landing, and for
those weeks nobody pays to cross. In deep winter the surface sets into loose
slabs over a channel that never freezes, and every year it drowns somebody who
took the slabs for ice.

The boat is a flat-bottomed lighter with a squared bow, a low rail, and one
short mast that carries the reading lantern above the crew's heads. It takes
eleven passengers, or four passengers and a cart. A crossing runs about fifty
minutes: the lighter cannot fight the current, so it is worked downstream on
the outbound leg and hauled back up the far bank on a shore rope at first
light. Two oak posts and a rail on each bank mark the only silt firm enough to
land on.

The Landing Ferry Company holds the crossing charter, a written grant from both
town councils. The charter fixes the toll, licenses the four crews allowed on
the water, sets the price the Company pays for oil, and requires that the
Standing Lamp burn whenever a licensed boat is out. The Standing Lamp is a
stone tower on the south bank with a lampreed burner behind an amber lens, and
it is the reference light every crew reads its channel against. The Company
keeps the tower, buys the oil, and takes a fifth of every toll for doing it.

The oil comes from the Low Marsh below Tallow Landing, where the cutters take
lampreed by hand in the four weeks before the bloom. Pressed reed oil is the
only fuel that burns the narrow amber the reading depends on; tallow and fish
oil burn wide and white and flatten the two colours into one. The cutters sell
to the Company at the charter price, and have asked three times to have it
raised.

Sela Fenn is the senior ferry master at the Landing and the best lamp-reader on
either bank. She has crossed in bloom water for nineteen years and calls the
channel aloud from the mast while her crew poles below her. Hesk Varrow keeps
the lamp on the north bank. He grew up in Kestwick, was refused a licence by
the Company twice, and now works for the households that pay to keep his tower
burning.

Two autumns ago Kestwick raised its own tower and lit it. The argument is
plain: the river touches both banks, the Company sits on the south one, and a
Kestwick farmer who wants to sell grain across the water should not have to buy
a southern licence to see where the channel lies. Forty-one households pay
weekly into the Kestwick Lamp Subscription for the oil.

Two lamps burning at once do not double the light. Amber thrown from opposite
banks crosses over the Long Grey, the mile-long sandbar in mid-river, and the
scattered colour there reads deep over water two feet deep. On the first night
both towers burned together, a licensed lighter went onto the Long Grey at
speed with nine aboard and a horse, broke its rail and its mast, and lost the
horse and one passenger. Each town's inquiry blamed the other town's lamp.

This year the flakeshell did not die back. The first hard frost came on time,
the water stayed opaque behind it, and the spring fords never opened. The
Sennow has been unreadable by daylight for fourteen months. The crossing is now
the only way over at any season, the Company has raised the toll twice under a
clause written for storm weeks, and the lampreed cut before the bloom was
measured for a season that was supposed to end.

Both towers are still burning. The Company will not license a Kestwick crew
while the north lamp stands, and Kestwick will not darken the north lamp while
the toll is set in the south. Sela Fenn has begun crossing an hour before full
dark, when a little daylight still lies on the water and neither lamp reads
true, because a channel she is guessing at is safer than a channel two lamps
have lied about. Neither bank has oil enough to burn both towers through
another bloom.
