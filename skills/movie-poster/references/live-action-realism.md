# Live-action theatrical realism

Read this reference only when the requested movie poster should look like a
photographed live-action production.

## The realism decision

Realism comes from a credible capture situation, not from the word
`photorealistic`. Establish what stood in front of the camera:

1. a plausible location at a believable scale;
2. a person with a specific reason to be there;
3. clothes and objects produced by that person's actual work or circumstances;
4. a camera position and lens that could photograph the scene; and
5. natural light, weather, skin, fabric, and material response.

Prefer a physically modest but exact place over an enormous environment. A
harbor, orchard, kitchen, hearing room, corridor, loading yard, or roadside can
carry a whole film when the human action is specific.

## Contrivance test

Before prompting, ask:

- Would the subject still perform this action if no poster camera were present?
- Does every visible prop exist because of the story, or because the poster
  needed a symbol?
- Does the location follow from the synopsis without enlarging its scale?
- Is the image compelling through behavior, consequence, and observation even
  after decorative emblems are removed?
- Could this be a production still from the film rather than a diagram of its
  lore?

If not, revise the selected moment or the synopsis. Do not solve contrivance by
adding atmosphere, ruins, crowds, uniforms, or mythology.

## Prompt decisions

There is no fixed prompt layout. Make the decisions that establish a credible
photograph explicit when they matter:

- the source-supported story moment and real reason the subject is present;
- the location, physical scale, and usable production design;
- a fictional non-celebrity subject's action, expression, practical dress, and
  story-owned objects;
- a credible camera position, lens behavior, depth, and human/environment
  hierarchy;
- natural skin, fabric, material response, weather, and practical light;
- whether the image is one coherent scene or another user-chosen theatrical
  design; and
- exact title copy plus exclusions for unrelated text and marks.

Use negative direction only for likely or observed failures. For a restrained
live-action poster, useful distinctions may include `professionally shot
location photography, not illustration, matte painting, game concept art, or
obvious CGI`. Do not automatically accumulate a universal avoid list, and do
not suppress spectacle when the approved film genuinely requires it.

## Observable review

Review the pixels at original detail:

- Face, hands, hair, and body should survive ordinary photographic scrutiny.
- Lens perspective and depth should make spatial sense.
- Architecture and infrastructure should be usable, not decorative geometry.
- Rain, dirt, workwear, paper, tools, and masonry should respond consistently
  to the same light and weather.
- The person should not become an action hero merely because they are nearest
  the camera.
- The setting must remain legible enough to express the story without becoming
  a panoramic game level.
- Typography must be correctly spelled, intentional, and isolated from stray
  generated text.

When the result looks like concept art, regenerate from a fresh prompt that
states `professionally shot live-action location photography`, narrows the
physical scale, names a credible lens and viewpoint, requests one coherent
scene, and explicitly excludes illustration, matte painting, game art, and
obvious CGI. Do not use the failed image as a visual reference unless the user
wants to preserve part of it.
